Wheely, the mascot of this site, learned to drive from A to B with reinforcement learning. The policy has 350 parameters and runs in the browser on the [Playground](/playground), where you can draw walls and see the network's activations.

## Setup

Wheely observes five distance rays (−60° to +60°), the direction and distance to B, and its previous wheel command. It outputs the left and right wheel speeds 15 times a second. The network is 10 → 12 → 12 → 4 → 2.

The simulator is written in Python with NumPy: a circular differential-drive robot (radius 0.3, top speed 1.2 units/s), walls stored as capsules, physics at 30 Hz and the policy at 15 Hz. Training layouts are generated randomly in five categories: open fields, posts and bars, freehand scribbles, wobbly mazes, and mixes of these. A sixth category, traps (U-shaped pockets facing the start), is used only for testing.

## Policy and training

The actor is a Gaussian policy. The network outputs the mean wheel commands, and during training actions are sampled around that mean with a learned, state-independent standard deviation. At test time and in the browser it acts deterministically, using the mean clipped to [−1, 1]. The critic is a separate 64-64 tanh network that also receives inputs the actor never sees: the shortest-path distance to B, eight rays covering all directions, and the time left. This asymmetric actor-critic setup gives the critic better value estimates without changing what is deployed.

The reward per policy step is the progress along the shortest path to B, measured on a distance field computed with Dijkstra's algorithm over a grid inflated by the robot radius. There is a cost of 0.01 per step, 0.05 per step in wall contact, and +10 for reaching B. An episode ends at B, or after three times the shortest-path driving time plus 10 s.

Training uses PPO from Stable-Baselines3 with 96 parallel environments:

The cap on the exploration noise means the final policy cannot rely on noise to get unstuck. A five-stage curriculum moves from open fields to obstacles, scribbles, mazes and finally a mix, advancing when the success rate over the last 1,000 episodes reaches 80%. Every 1M steps the deterministic policy is evaluated on 100 validation layouts per category; training keeps the best checkpoint and stops after four evaluations in the final stage without improvement. The final run stopped at 12M of a 30M-step budget, with the best checkpoint at 6M, after about 32 minutes on an 8-core CPU.

Each version was then tested on a separate set of 1,000 unseen layouts per category and compared with a heuristic policy (By-the-Book) that uses the same inputs.

## Failures

<Video src={moonwalker} poster={moonwalkerPoster.src} width={960} height={600} label="Moonwalker reversing towards B on an empty field" caption="Moonwalker takes 18 s for a trip the final version does in 6 s." />

The first version was allowed to reverse. It learned to drive backwards whenever B was behind it, but its rays only face forward, so it reversed into walls it could not see. Exploration noise during training hid the problem, and the deterministic policy got worse as training went on. Removing reversing fixed it; a round robot can always turn on the spot instead.

<Video src={scaredy} poster={scaredyPoster.src} width={960} height={600} label="Scaredy-Wheely stopping in front of the first wall of the slalom" caption="Scaredy-Wheely stops after a few seconds and does not move again." />

A later version had a large penalty for touching walls. Once mazes were added to training, standing still scored better than risking contact, and it froze in 24% of empty arenas. A smaller penalty fixed it.

## Results

Larger networks did not help: from 6-6 to 64-64 hidden units, maze success stayed between 43% and 46%. Adding rays that cover the back (Owl Eyes, seven rays) raised trap success from 18% to 64%. Giving the network its previous wheel command as an input helped the most. It reduced oscillation, and that version matches or beats the heuristic policy in 6 of 7 categories.

A recurrent (GRU) policy, a longer discount horizon, a bonus for arriving early and a critic with access to the true shortest path gave little or no improvement.

## Mazes

Mazes are the one category where the heuristic policy is better: 69% against 60%. The difference depends on how much the path turns:

84% of Wheely's maze failures happen in the first quarter of the path, where it moves back and forth in corridors that lead away from B. It heads for the goal reliably but does not commit to long detours, which a one-step memory cannot fix. The Trap layout on the Playground shows this.

Each configuration was trained with a single seed, so small differences between versions are within noise. Trap success varied between 32% and 76% across near-identical runs.

## Browser port

The simulator is ported to TypeScript and tested against the Python version; over 486-step episodes the positions agree to within 10⁻¹⁴. The demo is under 20 KB and draws each neuron's strongest incoming signal as it drives.
